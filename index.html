Know Your Rig

Hitch and lock

Coupler locked and pinned, safety chains crossed, and the breakaway cable clipped to the truck, not the chains.

Lights and brakes

Plug in the 7-pin cord, then test running lights, turn signals, and trailer brakes before every trip.

Weight-distribution hitch

If you have one, set it up correctly so the truck and trailer ride level.

Height and tires

Know your overall height. Check tire pressure cold before every trip.

Site arrival

(Pick the site, then chock the wheels before you unhitch.)

When you pull into a site you think you want, the first thing to do is shut off the breakers on the power pedestal. Then plug your surge protector into the pedestal, turn the breaker back on, and make sure the power is good. If the power is good, shut the breaker back off, plug the trailer's power cord into the surge protector, and then switch the breaker back on. Now go ahead and set up camp: chock the wheels before you unhitch. If the power is bad, ask for a different site before you unhitch.

Test the pedestal

(Use an RV surge protector to test the campground power pedestal before plugging in. It checks both legs if present and the polarity.)

Use a RV surge protector to test the campground power pedestal before plugging in. It checks both legs if present and the polarity.

Leveling

Level side to side first with blocks, then front to back with the tongue jack.

Stabilizers

Stabilizer jacks only steady the trailer. Never use them to lift it.

Slide and awning clearance

Check overhead and all around before running slides or the awning.

50-amp service

If your trailer is a 50-amp unit, it has 120/240 volt service with two 120-volt legs at 50 amps each, about 12,000 watts total, versus about 3,600 watts on a standard 30-amp hookup. On a full 50-amp pedestal you can usually run both A/Cs, the microwave, and the water heater together.

On a 30-amp site

If a site only has 30 amps, plug in with a 50-to-30 adapter. You only get one 30-amp leg, so run one A/C at a time and don't stack the microwave, electric water heater, and A/C. On a 15/20-amp household outlet, run lights and the fridge only.

Battery disconnect

(Know where the battery disconnect is, and keep the battery charged in storage.)

Know where the battery disconnect is, and keep the battery charged in storage. Even with the battery disconnect off, most RVs still have a parasitic drain, such as the LP detector.

City water hookup

(Use a pressure regulator and a drinking-water hose at the spigot.)

At the spigot, use three things: a pressure regulator, a water filter on the hose, and the correct drinking-water hose, which is usually white to show it's safe to drink from. Flush the spigot for a few seconds before connecting, keep the regulator at about 40 to 50 psi, and turn the water on slowly. Check every connection for leaks, inside and out, before you walk away.

Fresh tank and pump

(Know the difference between city water and filling the fresh tank and using the water pump.)

Even when you're hooked up to city water, keep at least a quarter tank in the fresh water tank. Travel with that quarter tank at the start and the end of every trip, so you have water on board if you break down. Don't carry a full tank, though, because that's too much weight to drag around all the time.

Water heater basics

(Fill the water heater with water before turning it on. Know where the bypass valve is.)

With a tank water heater, just turn it on about ten minutes before you want hot water. With an instant water heater, set the temperature on its control panel instead. Never mix hot and cold at the faucet to cool the water down, because the heater will shut off and you'll lose hot water. Run the hot side wide open and let the heater work at full speed.

Propane tanks

(Open the tanks slowly, and learn how the auto-changeover regulator works.)

With two tanks and an auto-changeover regulator, slowly open both tanks and point the changeover dial at one of them. When that tank runs empty, often around 2 or 3 in the morning when the heat quits, the regulator switches to the other tank on its own. That's why both stay open. The indicator turns red to show the tank the dial points to is empty. Turn the dial to the full tank and the indicator goes green. Then disconnect the empty tank, get it filled, and hook it back up. When the second tank runs out, the indicator turns red again and the regulator switches to the refilled tank.

Gas appliances

Light the fridge, furnace, and water heater on gas. Turn propane off while refueling, and in tunnels or on ferries where required.

Dumping order

Dump black first, then gray to rinse the hose.

Black valve closed

(At a campsite, keep the black valve closed until the tank is at least two-thirds full.)

At a campsite, keep the black valve closed until the tank is at least two-thirds full. After you dump, add about six gallons of water back into the black tank so there's always water in it.

Toilet paper and treatment

(Use RV toilet paper and holding-tank treatment.)

You don't have to use RV toilet paper, but regular paper means taking care of the black tank. Before a trip, put about six gallons of water in it so it sloshes on the road and solids land in water instead of piling up into a pyramid. With that water in the tank, you don't need to fill the bowl each time. Just step on the foot flap. On the way home, if you can dump at home, add a quarter cup of laundry detergent before leaving the campsite. Use laundry detergent, not sink soap, which makes too many bubbles. At home, dump and rinse the tank, then fill it nearly full through the Santa flush inlet and dump again to clear the soapy water. It's extra work, but it keeps the sensors working.

Sewer hygiene

Wear gloves, and keep the sewer hose separate from the fresh-water hose.

Fridge pre-cool

(Start the fridge 12 to 24 hours before the trip and load it with cold food.)

A 12-volt refrigerator doesn't need to be turned on the night before. Turn it on about four hours before you load the food. An absorption refrigerator, which runs on propane or 120 volts, has to be turned on the night before you leave so it's cold before anything goes in. If you load an absorption fridge before it has cooled down overnight, it will never get down to temperature and the food will spoil.

Slide use

(Keep slides clear of obstacles, and check the top of the slides for debris before closing them.)

Before putting the slides out at a campsite, make sure they're clear of obstacles. Before bringing them in, always find a way to look on top of the slides for branches, acorns, or anything else that has landed there. Closing a slide with debris on top will tear up the slide seals, and then they'll have to be replaced.

Awning use

(Leave clearance on the passenger side, tilt the awning so rain drains, make sure it's level before you retract it, and never leave it out in wind.)

When you pull into a site, leave plenty of room on the passenger side for the awning to go out. Most newer awnings let you pull down one arm to tilt the awning slightly, so rain runs off that lower corner instead of pooling on top. Before bringing it in, level it back out, then retract it all the way so it locks in place. Never leave the campsite with the awning out, because that's when a strong wind will tear it up.

Maintenance and Trouble Spots

Roof inspection

Inspect the roof and sealant twice a year. Never use silicone on the roof; use matching self-leveling lap sealant.

Slide seals

(Clean and lubricate the slide seals regularly.)

Every time you put the slides out, check the side slide seals and make sure none of them are folded back in. Those seals are what keep rain out of the RV. Use the handle of the broom you keep in the RV: slip it behind the side seal and push up to pop the blade out into its outward position. If a seal stays folded in during a hard rain, water gets where it shouldn't, and most of the time it ends up on the floor. An RV floor can only take getting wet in the same spot two or three times before it starts to break down. Clean and lubricate the slide seals regularly.

Window and door seals

(Check the seals and caulk around windows and doors for cracks or gaps.)

Check the seals and caulk around windows and doors for cracks or gaps. Look them over every six months.

Tire care

(Check tire date codes, and cover the tires from the sun when parked.)

Check tire date codes, and cover the tires from the sun when parked. Replace the tires every six years, based on the date code on the side of the tire.

Lug nut torque

(Re-torque lug nuts after the first 50 miles and after any tire service.)

After any tire service, re-torque the lug nuts with a torque wrench after about 50 miles. Don't over-tighten them, because overtightened studs can snap and the wheel can come off while you're driving. Know your lug nut torque spec, and carry a torque wrench with the correct socket, plus a 3-inch extension if you need one.

Bearings and brakes

(Service the wheel bearings and brakes on the manufacturer's schedule.)

Have the wheel bearings repacked and the brakes inspected every 12 months or 12,000 miles, whichever comes first. Replace the grease seals each time the hubs come off.

Water heater service

Flush the water heater, and check the anode rod if it's a Suburban.

Battery care

Check the battery water level and keep it charged.

Winterize

Winterize before the first freeze; in North Texas that's usually December through February.

Pre-trip safety check

(Test the smoke, CO, and LP detectors and check the extinguisher gauge every month.)

When you load up for each camping trip, test the smoke, CO, and LP detectors and check the extinguisher gauge.

Winterizing your unit

(Winterize before the first freeze: fill the water heater before turning it on, know the bypass valve, include the ice maker, and get a mobile RV tech if you are unsure.)

Fill before turning on

(Fill the water heater with water before turning it on.)

Make sure there's water in the water heater before you turn it back on.

Bypass valve

(Know where the bypass valve is.)

Know where the water heater bypass valve is.

Ice maker

(Include the ice maker line when you winterize.)

If your refrigerator has an ice maker, include it when you winterize. It's one of the most commonly missed spots.

Get help if unsure

(If you're not sure how to winterize, have a mobile RV tech do it.)

If you're not sure of how to properly winterize, have a mobile RV tech do it. That way you don't find water leaks and damage right before your first trip after a freeze.

Don't-Forget List

Water gear

(Drinking-water hose, pressure regulator, and water filter.)

A white drinking-water hose, a pressure regulator, a water filter, and a separate gray hose for the Santa flush on the black tank.

Sewer gear

(Sewer hose with fittings, disposable gloves, and RV toilet paper.)

Sewer hose with fittings, disposable gloves, and toilet paper.

Power gear

A 50-amp surge protector, a 50A-to-30A dog bone adapter, a 50A-to-15/20A adapter, and a 50A-rated extension cord if you need extra reach.

Leveling gear

(Leveling blocks, wheel chocks, and a tire pressure gauge.)

Andersen Camper Levelers (curved drive-on levelers with a chock), wheel chocks, and a tire pressure gauge.

Tools and lights

(Basic tool kit, flashlight or headlamp, and spare fuses.)

Basic tool kit, flashlight or headlamp, and a color-coded assortment of blade fuses in all the standard sizes.

Propane supply

Extra propane, or know where to refill along your route.

Safety kit

(First aid kit and a spare fire extinguisher.)

First aid kit.

Paperwork

Owner's manuals, registration, insurance card, and campground reservations.

Home basics

Kitchen basics, bedding for the bunks, and a door mat.

Nice-to-Have Purchases

Electronic leveling sensor

A Bluetooth leveling box (for example, LevelMate Pro) mounts inside the trailer and shows on your phone how far out of level you are side to side and front to back, plus how many inches of block to use, so you can pull onto the blocks right the first time.

Tire pressure monitor

A TPMS with sensors on each trailer tire shows pressure and temperature on a dash screen and warns you of a slow leak or blowout before it does damage.

Backup camera

A wireless rear-view camera on the back of the trailer makes backing into sites and changing lanes much safer.

Wheel chocks and X-chocks

X-chocks that wedge between the tandem tires stop the trailer from rocking and shifting when parked.

Stackable leveling blocks

Interlocking blocks (like Lynx Levelers) handle uneven sites and give the stabilizer jacks a solid base.

Water pressure gauge

A regulator with a built-in gauge lets you see and adjust campground water pressure.

Inline water filter

A disposable inline filter at the hose keeps sediment out of your tanks and improves drinking water.

Sewer hose support

A collapsible sewer hose support keeps the hose sloped downhill so the tanks drain fully.

Clear sewer elbow

A clear elbow fitting lets you see when the tank is empty and the rinse water runs clear.

Black tank flush wand

A flush wand or rinser cleans the black tank and helps keep the sensors reading correctly.

Portable generator

An inverter generator (about 3,500 watts or more to run one A/C) gives you power when boondocking or during an outage. Never run it near windows or vents.

Outdoor mat

A large outdoor rug under the awning keeps dirt and grass out of the trailer.

Camp chairs and table

Folding chairs and a small table make the campsite comfortable.

Outdoor griddle or grill

A portable propane griddle or grill keeps cooking heat and smell outside.

Awning LED lights

Low-power LED rope lights under the awning light the campsite at night.

Bed and storage organizers

Bins, drawer liners, and shelf risers keep cabinets from spilling items on the road.

Spare fuses and bulbs

(Keep spare RV fuses, 12-volt LED bulbs, and a multimeter handy.)

Keep spare RV fuses, 12-volt LED bulbs, and a multimeter handy. If you have an instant water heater, also carry a spare of the glass fuse inside it. Unscrew the one that's in there now to see its amperage, and match it.

Roadside assistance plan

An RV-specific roadside plan (Good Sam, Coach-Net, or your insurer) covers towing a trailer, which car plans often don't.

Campground app

An app like Campendium or RV Life helps find campgrounds, dump stations, and RV-safe routes.
